The sixty-second version

This is genuinely enough to come home with good footage.

  1. Twist the little screen clockwise to turn the camera on. Twist it back the other way to turn it off.

    The head will wobble and level itself for a second or two. That's normal. Let it settle.

  2. Press the record button once to start. Press it once to stop.

    You'll see a red border and a timer counting up. No red border, no recording — check every time.

  3. Hold each shot for a slow count of ten. Longer than feels natural. Then stop.

    Ten-second clips cut together. Three-second clips do not.

  4. Walk with it instead of waving it. Move your feet, keep your wrist still, and let the camera do the smoothing.

  5. Don't change any settings. They're already right for everything you'll shoot.

Three ways to break it

Water. It is not waterproof. Rain, spray at a waterfall, a spilled drink — any of those can end it. Sand and dust. They get into the moving head and it never moves the same again. Forcing the head by hand. Never push, twist or hold the camera head to point it. Point the whole camera instead.

When it's not in your hand, the cover goes on. The cover is what holds the head still in your bag.

The camera

Eight things on the body

There are only two buttons. Everything else is the touchscreen.

1
The rotating screen. This is the power switch. Twist clockwise to turn on, counter-clockwise to turn off. Twisting it also flips the picture between wide (normal) and tall (for Instagram and TikTok).
2
The head. It floats on motors — that's why your footage looks smooth. Never push it with your finger, and never hold it still while it's on.
3
The lens. Fingerprints are the single most common reason holiday footage looks hazy. Wipe it on a clean soft t‑shirt before you shoot.
4
The joystick. Nudge it to tilt and swivel the camera without moving your hand. Pressing it does useful things — see below.
5
The record button. One press starts, one press stops. Holding it for a second shuts the camera down.
6
The microphones. Right where your fingers naturally sit. Keep them clear, or everything sounds muffled.
7
USB‑C. Charges from any phone charger, laptop or power bank. Same cable as most modern phones.
8
The memory card. Already in, already empty. Don't take it out and don't format it — that's the whole trip gone.

If you ever get confused about where the camera is pointing, double-press the joystick. It's the reset button for everything gimbal-related.

The menu

Four swipes and two taps

That's the entire interface. You'll mostly use the taps.

The two taps that actually matter

  • Tap once on a face or an object. The camera focuses there and sets the brightness for it. Use this constantly — if someone looks like a dark silhouette against a bright sky, tap their face and they'll brighten up.
  • Double-tap a person. The camera locks onto them and follows them around the frame by itself. Brilliant for someone walking ahead of you, dancing, or riding a horse. Single-press the joystick to let go.
Getting back to normal

Lost in a menu? Swipe back the way you came, or just twist the screen off and on again. You cannot break anything by pressing things — you can only change a setting, and the next section tells you which ones matter.

Three changes worth making

These are the only ones worth learning. Each one is reversible in a few seconds.

A Shooting for Instagram or TikTok

Twist the screen counter-clockwise until the picture goes tall. Shoot vertical properly rather than cropping a wide shot later — it looks better and you keep all the detail.

twist screen anticlockwise → picture goes tall → twist back for normal

B Slow motion

Swipe up, choose Slow Motion. Worth it for waterfalls, street performers, horses, someone jumping into water, wine being poured. Not worth it for anything else — there's no sound in slow motion and the files are huge.

swipe up → Slow Motion → shoot 5–8 seconds → swipe up → Video to go back

C Time-lapse of clouds or a busy street

Swipe up and pick Timelapse. Prop the camera somewhere solid, press record, and leave it for at least five minutes. Clouds over the Andes and a plaza at dusk both look great. It eats battery, so don't do it on a day you'll need the camera later.

swipe up → Timelapse → prop it up, don't hold it → 5–10 min minimum

One setting to leave completely alone

If you find anything called D‑Log M or 10‑bit, back out of it. It makes footage look flat and grey and needs editing software to fix. The Normal setting is already right.

Shooting

Ten shots that will carry the week

Get two or three of these each day and you'll come home with something worth watching. Every one of them is: press record, do the thing for ten seconds, press stop.

01 The walk-in

Hold the camera at chest height and walk into a place — through a doorway, into a market, down a street. Walk slowly and normally. The camera does the rest.

chest height · walk at half your normal speed · 10–15 sec

02 The reveal

Start pointed at something close and small — a glass, a boot, a hand on a railing — then slowly lift the camera to show the view behind it. Two seconds on the detail, then move.

detail for 2 sec · lift slowly over 4 sec · hold the view 5 sec

03 The follow

Double-tap whoever is walking ahead of you. The camera locks on and keeps them in frame while you walk behind. This is the shot that looks most like it was filmed by someone who knows what they're doing.

double-tap the person · walk behind them · joystick press once to release

04 The table

Food, wine, coffee, hands. Put the camera low and close — almost on the table — and drift it slowly past the plate or glass. Get close enough that it feels slightly too close.

low & close · drift sideways, don't zoom · tap the plate to focus

05 The street, held low

Down at waist height, camera pointing forward, just walking. People behave normally because it doesn't look like filming. Great in Buenos Aires.

waist height · keep walking · 15 sec · don't stop to check the screen

06 Talking to camera

Triple-press the joystick so it faces you, hold it at arm's length slightly above your eye line, and talk. Clip the mic on if there's any wind or noise. Say where you are and what just happened — future-you will be glad.

joystick ×3 · arm's length, slightly above eyes · mic on if outdoors

07 The big landscape

For mountains, steppe, vineyards. Keep the horizon straight using the grid lines, put it near the top or bottom of the frame rather than the middle, and hold completely still for fifteen seconds. Resist the urge to pan around.

horizon on a grid line · absolutely still · 15 sec · no panning

08 The slow pan

If you must move across a view, use the joystick rather than turning your body — nudge it gently to one side and let it drift. Far smoother than anything you can do by hand.

joystick nudged and held · one direction only · 8–10 sec

09 Night and neon

The camera is genuinely good in the dark. Two rules: hold it dead still (any movement smears), and find a light source on people's faces — a shop window, a candle, a streetlight — rather than shooting against one.

still as possible · light in front of faces, not behind · tap the face to brighten

10 The one you'd forget

The bus window, the dog in the street, a hand pouring, feet on gravel, a menu board. Ten seconds each. These are the shots that make everything else feel like a place instead of a postcard.

whatever's in front of you · 10 sec · shoot more of these than you think

Habits

The eight things beginners get wrong

Fixing these matters more than any setting on the camera.

  • Hold every shot for ten seconds. Count it out loud in your head. Short clips are unusable; you can always cut a long one down.
  • Move your feet, not your wrist. Flicking the camera around ruins a shot faster than anything else.
  • Give it two seconds before and after. Press record, wait, then start moving. Stop moving, wait, then press stop.
  • Don't zoom. It's a fake zoom that throws away detail. Walk closer instead.
  • Don't cover the microphones with your fingers, and check the lens isn't under your thumb before every shot.
  • Check the red border. The classic disaster is filming a whole afternoon while the camera was off, and pressing record while it was on. Glance at the timer.
  • Turn your back to the wind. Or get very close to whoever is talking. Wind is the number one thing that makes travel footage unwatchable.
  • Shoot more than feels sensible. A week is maybe 200 clips. You'll use 30. That's a normal, healthy ratio.

Sound

The wireless mics

You have two little clip-on microphones and a charging case. The camera's own mics are fine for general noise and atmosphere; the clip-on is for when someone is talking and it matters — an interview, a piece to camera, a guide explaining something, anything outdoors with wind.

Connecting one — first time only

  1. Turn the camera on.

  2. Swipe down on the camera screen and find Wireless Mic. Tap TX1.

  3. On the little mic, hold the link button — the one marked with a chain symbol, just above the power button — for about three seconds, until its light flashes blue and green.

  4. The camera says it's connected. Done — it will reconnect on its own from now on. For the second mic, repeat with TX2.

Using them

  • Clip it high. Collar, lapel, the neckline of a t‑shirt — about a hand's width below the chin.
  • Outdoors, put the furry windscreen on it. It looks silly and it is the difference between usable and unusable.
  • Two mics = two people. Use TX1 and TX2 for a conversation.
  • Press the mic's power button once to toggle noise cancelling. Good in a loud street, but if a voice starts sounding thin or underwater, turn it back off.
  • Charge them in the case. They live in it, they charge in it, and the case charges over USB‑C. They last a long time — days, not hours.
  • Don't bury it under clothing or let a necklace, zip or seatbelt rub against it.
Quick check

Before an important bit of talking: record five seconds, swipe right to play it back, and listen. Five seconds now beats discovering it in Miami.

Logistics

Battery, card, plugs

Battery

Expect somewhere around an hour and a half to two hours of actual recording on a full charge — that's a full day of normal travel filming, since you're shooting ten seconds at a time, not continuously. It charges fast: fifteen or twenty minutes on a decent charger gets you most of the way back.

  • Top it up over lunch. Any power bank, any phone charger, same USB‑C cable as your phone.
  • Cold kills it. In Patagonia or anywhere high, keep the camera in an inside jacket pocket between shots, not an outside one. The battery indicator will lie to you in the cold and then recover once it warms up.
  • Turn it off between shots if you're stretching a day — the screen is what drains it.

The memory card

It's already in the camera and empty. Roughly: a minute of filming is a bit under a gigabyte, so there's room for hours — far more than a week of sensible shooting. To check what's left, swipe down and look at the storage number.

The one unrecoverable mistake

Never choose Format, and don't delete clips to make space. If you somehow do run out, tell Caleb and he'll sort it remotely — there's a spare card and it's a thirty-second swap.

Argentina specifics

  • Plugs are different. Argentina uses a slanted three-pin socket. Bring a travel adapter — the charger itself handles the voltage fine, you just need the shape to fit.
  • The sun is strong. Don't leave the camera on a dashboard or a sunny table. If it gets hot it'll warn you; move it into shade for a few minutes and it's fine.
  • Set the time zone if the camera asks — Argentina is UTC−3 — so the clips are stamped correctly.
  • Don't check it into hold luggage. Carry-on only, with the cover on.

Troubleshooting

If something goes wrong

Almost everything here is fixed by twisting the screen off and back on.

It won't turn on

It's flat. Plug it into anything USB‑C and give it fifteen minutes before deciding it's broken — a fully drained battery takes a moment to show any sign of life.

The head is tilted, drifting or pointing at the floor

First: double-press the joystick. That re-centres it and solves it nine times out of ten.

If it's still crooked, twist the screen off, put the cover on, wait ten seconds, take the cover off and twist it back on — that makes it re-level itself. If it's still wrong, swipe down, find the gimbal settings and run the auto-calibration.

The screen has frozen

Hold the record button down for a few seconds to force it off, then twist the screen to start it again.

The footage looks too dark or blown out

Tap on the thing you care about — a face, the food, the mountain. The camera re-does the brightness for that spot. If you're shooting someone against a bright sky and they're a silhouette, tapping their face fixes it instantly.

Everything looks slightly hazy or soft

Fingerprint on the lens. Breathe on it and wipe with a clean soft cloth or t‑shirt. Not your sleeve if you've been near sand.

The sound is muffled, or the mic isn't working

Check your fingers aren't over the microphone holes on the camera. If you're using a clip-on: is it switched on, is it actually connected (swipe down → Wireless Mic), and is it buried under a coat?

If a voice sounds thin or underwater, press the mic's power button once to turn noise cancelling off.

It says the card is full or missing

Turn it off and on first. If it still says missing, there's a spare card in the case — but message Caleb before you swap anything, and do not format whatever is in there.

It got wet or sandy

Turn it off immediately, don't try to dry it with heat, put the cover on and message Caleb. Do not keep shooting to see if it still works.
